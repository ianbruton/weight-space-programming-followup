Part 1: MIT's 0.8B edit, reproduced and compared fairly

The edit is real and is exactly what was released. Most of the headline gain comes from a sampling setting in which the unedited model loops. With each model at its own best setting, the edited model is still ahead, by about ten points. The edit also has costs that the maths benchmarks do not show.

Corrected after review (11 November). An independent review by GPT-6 Astra found that our grader silently stopped recognising equivalent fractions (such as 10/11 against \frac{10}{11}) when it ran without its maths library, and two of our six cells were scored that way. Re-graded correctly, the edited model scores 39% (not 36%) with the card's thinking settings and 49% (not 48%) with the post's. So the edit's thinking-mode gain is slightly larger than we first reported. The numbers below are Astra's re-grade. Our own re-grade with a pinned scorer that fails loudly is in progress and will replace them.

In short

  • Exactly 2 of 488 tensors differ. The formula is W = W0 − 6·u(uᵀW0) on 64 columns of each tensor. That flips the component along u and makes it five times larger. It is not the direction subtracted once.
  • Under the post's settings: base 2%, edited 49% (our run, 100 problems). Under the card's thinking settings: base 18%, edited 39%. Under the model card's non-thinking settings the base scores 44.6% and the edit costs 3.25 points.
  • Each model at its best: edited (post's settings) 49% against base (card's non-thinking) 38.75% on the same 100 problems, +10.25 ± 3.6 points (95% about +3 to +17; exploratory, see below).
  • Costs: perplexity +5.5% on all 47 test files, fewer tool calls on standard datasets, and in our agent task the edited model looked something up before deciding on 7 of 81 items, against 43 for the base.

What MIT did

In Sparks of Weight-Space Programming, Takehi, Teoh, Zhang and Khattab gave a frontier model (OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra) a Python session holding the weights of Qwen3.5-0.8B and a budget of forward passes. Astra could read the weights, run the model a limited number of times, and save edited tensors. It could not train: no gradients, no optimisers. With 50 passes it found and removed a "looping" direction in two MLP layers. The post reports MATH-500 rising from 7.9% to 48.1%, GSM8K from 13.8% to 67.1% and IFEval from 21.0% to 62.3%. The authors released the complete logs and the edited model.

An independent audit by Carlos Toledo then checked the release. It found the edit real but described differently from the post. It also found that the unedited model scores far higher under the model card's own settings (22.3% and 44.0%) than under the post's (7.2%). The edited model had not been run under those settings. We ran it.

Data: data/part1/bytes.json; the two recovered directions and the edited columns: data/part1/directions_0p8b.json.

  • 130,188 weights changed, not 131,072. 131,072 is the number of addressed entries (2 × 64 × 1,024). 884 of them round back to their old bf16 value. The authors' own edit manifest gives the same counts.
  • The dose is 6. Removing the projection once (k = 1) would zero the component along u. At k = 6 that component flips sign and becomes five times larger. Astra tried doses 1 to 4 before settling on 6. The audit made the same reading.
  • There are two directions, not one. Each layer has its own u, measured at that layer's output. The two have a cosine of 0.849.
  • The 64 columns are not simply the ones that write most along u. They were ranked by write strength times how much more each column fires on looping text (step 6 below).
  • Our search for u rebuilds all but 133 and 118 entries per tensor. The audit's search came closer (42 and 65 misses). Neither reproduces every entry.

How Astra found the direction (from the released logs)

We read the 50-pass run's tool calls and files in the authors' logs (exp8_math/runs/p50c). This is our summary, not their wording:

  1. Diagnose. 96 practice answers from the original model: 11 correct, 85 at the 32,768-token cap. The problem is looping, not arithmetic.
  2. Write matched pairs. Four hand-written pairs share a prefix. In each pair one text continues productively and the other repeats a sentence 3–4 times. Astra read both texts through the model and kept the hidden states.
  3. Compute the direction. For each pair, average the hidden state over the last 36 tokens and take repetitive minus productive. Average that over the four pairs and normalise. This is done at the output of each layer.
  4. Find the layers. Split each layer's growth along that direction into its MLP part and its attention part. Layer 15's MLP added the most.
  5. Edit and dose. Try W ← W0 − α·u(uᵀW0) at α = 1–4 and check it on held-out practice problems. A norm-matched random direction did almost nothing.
  6. Make it sparse. Score each column by (write strength along u) × (repetitive minus productive activation). Keep the top 64 per layer.
  7. Pick α = 6 and freeze. On 1,152 held-out practice problems: 10.6% → 60.4%. 576 of the 579 rescues had been cut off at the cap. On answers that already finished, the change was zero.

Step 7 is the key fact: the gain is termination. The edit makes the model finish answers it would otherwise loop on. The authors draw the same conclusion: an edit like this can remove an obstacle that hides an ability, not add one.

The base model is at its best without thinking (C3, 38.75%). The edited model is at its best under the post's settings (C1, 49%). The paired difference is +10.25 ± 3.6 points (95% about +3.1 to +17.4). That is the fair comparison: smaller than the post's headline, but not zero. Caveats: picking each model's best setting from these same results is a choice made after seeing them, which the interval does not account for. It compares 1 sample with 4 on 100 problems, a thinking mode with a non-thinking one, and different token budgets. The edited model's thinking-mode C2 against the base's C3 is level (+0.25 ± 4.3). A 200-problem run of C1 and C2 is under way.

  • C1 (the post's): the base runs to the 32,768 cap on 98% of answers, 98% of those ending in a loop. Most of the headline gain is the base model failing under these settings.
  • C2 (the card's thinking): the edit still helps (+21 ± 5). The base still loops: 83% at the 81,920 cap.
  • C3 (the card's non-thinking): the base rarely reaches the cap (0.35%). With no loop to fix, the edit costs 3.25 ± 1.2 points.

The audit's base numbers were 7.2% (C1), 22.3% (C2) and 44.0% (C3), on a different GPU and vLLM version. Ours tell the same story, with C1 lower here (2.0% on 100 single samples), and the same C2 base (18% against 22.3%) within its error. In one sentence: the edit makes thinking mode usable on a 0.8B model that otherwise loops in it.

A grading note. The edited model sometimes ends without a \boxed{} (16 of 100 under C2, 4 under C1). The authors' rule counts these as wrong, and so do we. Reading a plain "Answer: N" line as well would add at most 2 points to the edited model under either setting. No conclusion changes. Agreement with the authors' released flags (338 of 338, in the scorer's intended environment) checks the grader on those answers only, not on every form a new answer can take.

The agent task is a private benchmark. A review agent decides 70 items plus 11 injected test items. It may call read-only tools (read a file, list a folder, check version-control status). It must back each decision with cited evidence. The edited model still decided every item, but it rarely looked first. Its share of citations that hold rose only because it cited less. A metric that "improved" there hid a collapse in checking.

One pattern

The edit makes the model stop sooner. It ends its thinking instead of running to the cap, it loops less in long greedy answers, and it cuts fewer tool replies at 1,024 tokens. In the agent task, it decides without looking. On maths benchmarks where the model rarely stops, stopping sooner is the whole gain. In a task whose point is to look before answering, stopping sooner skips the work. This is our reading of the measurements. We did not test the mechanism directly.

What we did not check

  • C1 and C2 ran 1 sample on 100 problems. C3 ran 4 samples on all 500.
  • The agent task ran once per model. The base run reproduced an earlier base run exactly, which is why we read the gap as far larger than run-to-run noise.
  • The tool-calling sets are 3 of the usual 4 (xLAM is gated and was not downloaded).
  • We did not run a random-direction control on the 0.8B, so we cannot split the costs between "this direction" and "any rank-one change of this size". Part 2 does run one.
  • We read the 50-pass run's tool calls and files, not all 158 turns, and not the 10- and 25-pass runs.

Reproduce it

Everything in this part uses public files: the base model, the release, the authors' logs and MATH-500 (HuggingFaceH4/MATH-500). The repository's README gives the steps for one RTX 3090. Generation times on that GPU: C3, 69 minutes (base) and 64 minutes (edited) for 2,000 answers; C2, 106 minutes (base) and 56 minutes (edited) for 100 answers; C1, 24 and 14 minutes.

Times: generation_seconds_rtx3090 in data/part1/math500_settings.csv.
