Editing a model's weights by hand: a follow-up

In November 2026 four MIT CSAIL researchers showed a frontier model editing the weights of Qwen3.5-0.8B directly, with no training, and lifting its MATH-500 score from 7.9% to 48.1%. We checked the released weights byte by byte, ran both models under three sampling settings, measured what the edit costs elsewhere, and then tried the same recipe on a 4B model and on a 27B model for jobs of our own.

This is a follow-up, not a takedown. The authors released their logs, their edited model and a diff of the edit. That is why any of this could be checked. The edit is real and is exactly what they released. Our work adds a fair baseline, the costs, and two more models, one of which the recipe did not help.

Part 1: MIT's 0.8B edit, reproduced and compared fairly

The bytes match the release, but the edit applies six times the projection on one direction per layer, and it changes 130,188 weights, not 131,072. Under the post's settings the unedited model loops to the token cap. Looking across settings after the fact, the edited model's best is +6 ± 2.7 points above the base model's best on 200 problems, not +40. It costs 5.5% perplexity and some tool use, and in one run of our agent task it mostly stopped looking things up.

Part 2: our own 4B citation edit

The recipe transfers mechanically. A tiny edit raised the share of citations that hold from 89.7% to 93.1% and beat a random direction of the same size, but the test we planned came out "not shown", and on a second day's data the gain did not reach items that were new that day. It costs nothing measurable on tool calling, and a screen of 30 variants found nothing better.

Part 3: a 27B tool reflex

A negative result. The 27B's habit of calling a tool on a planted path is its ordinary checking habit, not obedience, and the recipe found a token, not a habit: the edit moved calls to other paths instead of removing them, and broke some answers. The check failed in every edited run.

How this was done

Everything ran on one RTX 3090 (24 GB). The experiments were designed and run by Claude agent sessions in Claude Code, under the author's direction. Each decision rule (which edit family, which dose, what counts as "it worked") was written down before the test it judged. Where we changed a plan after seeing a result, the pages say so.

All three parts were then reviewed by GPT-6 Astra, the model that found MIT's edit, working as a strict reviewer. It found a grading fault of ours (which had understated the edit's thinking-mode gain) and several overstatements, and it ranked what to try next. The corrections are marked on each page. Astra and Fable (Claude Fable 5.2, which ran the experiments) now work as a pair on each model size: Astra reviews and proposes, Fable decides what to run and writes its success rule first. Some of their follow-up runs were still queued when these pages were written; the pages say which.

Every number on these pages links to a file in the data folder. The scripts are in the repository's code/ folder. Part 1 can be reproduced from public files. Parts 2 and 3 use a private benchmark, so we publish the method, the edit directions and the aggregate results, not the benchmark itself.
